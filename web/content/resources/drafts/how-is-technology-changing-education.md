# How is technology changing education?

Technology is changing education in uneven, practical ways — not as a single revolution that arrives overnight. Some shifts are structural (who can access learning, how materials are distributed). Others are pedagogical (how feedback works, how practice happens between lessons). A few are noisy trends that will fade.

This article is for teachers, school leaders, and parents who want a clear reading of the change — especially as it affects English language learning — without pretending every classroom already looks like a demo video. For definitions and tool categories, start with [What is EdTech?](/resources/what-is-edtech).

The best public synthesis of the global debate remains UNESCO’s [2023 Global Education Monitoring Report — *Technology in education: A tool on whose terms?*](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/publication/technology). Its core argument still holds as AI accelerates: technology should support teacher-led learning, equity, and evidence — not replace them.

## The short answer

Technology is changing education by:

1. **Expanding access** to materials, models, and practice beyond the classroom walls  
2. **Compressing feedback loops** — students can get more attempts and faster signals  
3. **Shifting teacher work** toward design, facilitation, and diagnosis (and away from some repetitive prep)  
4. **Raising new risks** around attention, equity, privacy, and over-automation  
5. **Making “connected” workflows possible** — one activity used live, as homework, and for independent practice

The teachers who benefit most are not the ones who adopt every tool. They are the ones who pick a few changes that protect learning time and language goals.

## Five real shifts (not slogans)

### 1. Learning is less bound to one room and one hour

Recordings, shared links, asynchronous tasks, and mobile practice mean students can continue after the bell. For English learners, that matters: language needs repetition and spaced practice.

During COVID-era closures, remote models also exposed how many learners were left out without devices or connectivity — a finding the [GEM Report launch summary](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/articles/unesco-issues-urgent-call-appropriate-use-technology-education) puts in stark terms. Access gains are real; exclusion risks are real too.

**Classroom implication:** Design the *out-of-class* task as carefully as the live segment. A weak homework link wastes the best part of digital distribution.

Parents supporting home practice can start with structured pathways such as [English learning for kids at home](/english-learning-for-kids-at-home), and free practice libraries like [LearnEnglish Kids](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/).

### 2. Practice volume can rise — if the UX is simple

Digital activities can give every learner more turns than a single whole-class round-robin. That is a genuine change for speaking and vocabulary practice — *when join flows are simple and tasks are short*.

British Council materials on [maximising speaking opportunities in online lessons](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/maximising) and Cambridge’s [Teaching English online tips](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/teaching-online/) both emphasize interaction design over feature lists.

**Classroom implication:** Prefer tools kids can enter quickly (clear codes, minimal account friction) and tasks that fit a focused language objective.

See interactive examples on [ESL activities for kids](/esl-activities-for-kids) as those hubs publish.

### 3. Feedback is becoming more frequent — and more uneven in quality

Auto-scoring, speech prompts, and AI comments can increase feedback frequency. Quality still varies widely. A green checkmark is not the same as noticing a student never uses past tense in free production.

The OECD’s [Digital Education Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html) synthesizes emerging evidence that generative AI can improve *task success* while undermining learning if students outsource thinking. Feedback volume is not feedback quality.

**Classroom implication:** Keep teacher judgment at the center. Use tech for volume and first-pass signals; use humans for meaning, interaction, and goals.

### 4. Teacher labor is being redesigned, not erased

Lesson drafting, item generation, and media editing can be faster. Planning still requires professional choices: sequencing, level fit, cultural appropriateness, and assessment.

UNESCO’s [Guidance for generative AI in education and research](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research) calls for teacher capacity-building and institutional validation of tools — not “paste the prompt and ship.” For school digital-competence language shared with administrators, see [ISTE](https://www.iste.org/) and the OECD/EC [AILit framework for primary and secondary learners](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2026/06/empowering-learners-for-the-age-of-ai_2f8315e7/65cd27d4-en.pdf) (PDF).

**Classroom implication:** Use AI and templates to draft, then edit like an experienced teacher — especially for young learners.

### 5. Data is easier to collect — and easier to misuse

Platforms can show completion, accuracy, time-on-task, and mastery estimates. That can help groups and interventions. It can also create noise, false precision, or privacy problems.

GEM 2023 notes how few countries guarantee education data privacy in law and how often EdTech products can survey children. Privacy is part of instructional design now, not only IT policy.

**Classroom implication:** Track a few indicators tied to learning goals. Avoid dashboards that reward busywork.

## What is changing specifically for English teaching

Language education feels these shifts acutely because success depends on **input quality, interaction, and output**.

| Area | How technology helps | What still requires teaching craft |
|------|----------------------|------------------------------------|
| Input | Rich audio/video, graded readers, adaptive text | Choosing comprehensible, purposeful input |
| Interaction | Breakouts, live games, chat tasks | Designing real information gaps and turn-taking |
| Output | Recording, writing platforms, speech practice | Criteria, models, and meaningful audiences |
| Feedback | Instant item checks, some speech/writing aids | Corrective feedback that learners can use |
| Motivation | Games, streaks, pets/rewards (in some products) | Goals that feel worth caring about |

Online English teaching works best when technology serves a clear loop: create → teach live → assign → independent practice → review. That workflow is spelled out in [How to teach English online](/teach-english-online). For method detail, Cambridge’s [quick-start guide to teaching English online](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/schools/quick-start-guide-to-teaching-english-online.pdf) (PDF) remains one of the most usable free references.

## Equity: the change that decides whether progress is real

Technology can widen opportunity — or widen gaps.

Watch for:

- **Device and bandwidth gaps** between learners  
- **Home support differences** (who can sit with a child during practice)  
- **Language access for parents** on platforms and messages  
- **Age-appropriate privacy** and safe defaults for children  

A “powerful” tool that only works for well-resourced homes is not a whole-class solution. Design for the learner with the weakest connection and the least support first. TeachingEnglish’s guidance on [inclusion in remote teaching](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/inclusion) and [remote teaching with limited technology](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/publications/resource-books/remote-teaching-tips) is written for exactly those constraints.

## AI in education: useful, overclaimed, and uneven

AI is accelerating change in content drafting, tutoring experiments, translation, and personalized pathways. It is also producing confident errors, shallow tasks, and privacy questions. Sector journalism such as [EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/) is useful for tracking product claims; UNESCO and OECD reports are useful for filtering them.

A grounded stance for English teachers:

- **Use AI** for brainstorming examples, differentiating drafts, and generating extra practice items you will review  
- **Do not outsource** curriculum goals, child safety, or final accuracy  
- **Prefer products** where the teacher can inspect and edit materials before students see them  
- **Be transparent** with schools and families about when AI is involved  
- **Read the policy layer** — start with [UNESCO GenAI guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research) and the [OECD 2026 Outlook](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html)

Policy and research organizations consistently return to the same theme: technology should strengthen teaching and inclusion, not replace the relationship that makes learning human.

## What is *not* changing (important)

- Children still need relationships, routines, and belonging  
- Language still needs meaningful use, not only drills  
- Teachers still set goals and culture in the room (physical or virtual)  
- Assessment still needs to sample real ability, not only click-speed  
- Attention is still limited — more screens do not equal more learning  

If a proposed “transformation” ignores these constants, treat it as marketing. GEM 2023’s reminder is useful here: digital technology should complement face-to-face interaction with teachers, not substitute for it.

## How to adapt without burning out

A practical change plan for the next term:

1. **Pick one bottleneck** — prep time, speaking turns, homework completion, or visibility of progress  
2. **Choose one tool or workflow** that attacks that bottleneck  
3. **Define success** in learner terms (e.g., “every student records two sentences using today’s past forms”)  
4. **Run for four weeks** before adding anything else  
5. **Keep a stop-doing list** — remove a manual process when a digital one proves better  

For motivation and wellbeing while teaching remotely, see TeachingEnglish on [maintaining student motivation](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/maintaining) and [managing teacher well-being](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/managing).

## FAQ

### Is technology replacing teachers?

No credible classroom model replaces skilled teachers for language learning at scale. UNESCO’s GEM framing — technology as a *tool*, never a substitute for teacher-led instruction — is the right default. Technology changes *tasks* teachers spend time on; it does not remove the need for human guidance, especially with children.

### Should schools go fully online?

Most young learners still benefit from live human instruction and social learning. Online and blended formats are tools for access and practice — not automatic upgrades. See also the British Council / FutureLearn course [Teaching Young Learners Online](https://www.futurelearn.com/courses/teaching-young-learners-online).

### What should parents watch for?

Look for clear learning goals, reasonable screen time, simple routines, and teachers or platforms that can explain what a child practiced — not only that they earned points. [LearnEnglish Kids’ parents section](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/) is a strong free starting point for home support ideas.

### What is the biggest mistake schools make with EdTech?

Buying tools before defining the teaching problem, then training everyone on features instead of classroom routines. GEM 2023 also warns against underestimating long-term costs and over-trusting vendor evidence.
