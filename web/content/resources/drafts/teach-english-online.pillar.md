# How to teach English online to children

<!-- SEO H1 candidate (plan): How to teach English online to children -->

Teaching English online to kids works when the lesson has a clear language goal, fast participation, and a path for practice after class — not when the hour is a long lecture through a webcam.

This page walks through a practical workflow: **create → teach live → assign → practise → review**. It is written for teachers and schools. Parents supporting home learning should also see [English learning for kids at home](/english-learning-for-kids-at-home).

For the broader tool landscape, see [What is EdTech?](/resources/what-is-edtech). For free professional development on the same topic, the British Council’s [Teaching Young Learners Online](https://www.futurelearn.com/courses/teaching-young-learners-online) course and Cambridge’s [Teaching English online hub](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/teaching-online/) are excellent companions to this workflow.

## What “good” online English looks like for kids

Young learners need:

- Short activity cycles (not 20-minute monologues)
- Visible models and lots of repetition with variation
- Low-friction ways to join and take turns
- Movement between listen → say → play → show understanding
- Homework that feels like a continuation, not a surprise worksheet dump

Technology should make those things easier. If a tool adds login pain or unclear instructions, it is fighting the lesson. Cambridge’s [quick-start guide to teaching English online](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/schools/quick-start-guide-to-teaching-english-online.pdf) (PDF) stresses the same foundations: plan stages, group work, transitions, and feedback *before* you go live.

UNESCO’s [GEM Report on technology in education](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/publication/technology) is also useful framing for schools: digital tools should support teacher-led learning, not replace it — especially with children.

## The connected workflow (use this as your planning spine)

### 1. Create once

Build an interactive activity around one clear objective (e.g., “ask and answer about hobbies using *Do you…?* / *I…*”).

Include:

- Target language and example sentences
- Visuals kids can parse quickly
- A practice task with more than one attempt
- Notes for yourself on timing and differentiation

**Platform fit:** Author in a lesson/activity builder so the same piece can be reused live and as homework — instead of rebuilding slides, then a quiz, then a separate game.

Looking for ready-made practice patterns? Browse [LearnEnglish Kids](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/) and Cambridge [teacher resources](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/) for task shapes you can adapt.

### 2. Teach live

In the live session (video classroom or computer-assisted in-person class):

1. Warm up with known language  
2. Present the target with clear models  
3. Run controlled practice (choral → pairs → short game)  
4. Check understanding with a quick interactive task  
5. Preview the homework so students know what “done” looks like  

**Keep join simple.** Class codes and big buttons beat nested menus — especially for primary ages.

**Worth reading before your next live class:**

- TeachingEnglish — [Lesson planning for teaching live online](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/lesson)
- TeachingEnglish — [Zoom: top tips for online English teaching](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/zoom-top-tips)
- TeachingEnglish — [Maximising speaking opportunities in online lessons](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/maximising)

### 3. Assign homework

Send the *same* activity or a tight sibling task. Students should recognize the language from class.

Good online homework for kids:

- Has a visible finish line  
- Can be completed in 10–20 minutes  
- Does not require a parent to teach the grammar point from scratch  

TeachingEnglish’s [remote teaching tips](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/publications/resource-books/remote-teaching-tips) include practical sheets on helping parents and caregivers support home learning — useful when you send the first homework link of the term.

### 4. Guide independent practice

Between lessons, learners need spaced reps. Interactive practice, short stories, or grammar posters can carry the week — if the path is obvious.

Browse activity ideas on [ESL activities for kids](/esl-activities-for-kids), and point families to [LearnEnglish Kids](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/) for safe, free practice between your assigned tasks.

### 5. Review progress

Before the next live class, look for:

- Who completed practice  
- Which items caused errors  
- Whether the objective is ready to extend or needs reteaching  

Then open the next lesson with evidence, not guesswork. As AI scoring tools spread, remember the OECD [Digital Education Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html) caution: task completion with AI is not the same as learning. Keep human review in the loop.

## A sample online lesson structure (45–60 minutes)

| Phase | Time | What happens |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Connect | 3–5 min | Greetings, routine question, devices ready |
| Warm-up | 5 min | Known language game or flash review |
| Present | 7–10 min | Target language + clear examples |
| Practice | 10–15 min | Interactive controlled practice |
| Produce | 8–10 min | Freer speaking/writing with support |
| Check | 5 min | Quick interactive check / exit ticket |
| Assign | 3 min | Show homework screen; confirm “done” means |

Adjust times for age. Younger primary often needs even shorter cycles. For more staging ideas, see TeachingEnglish’s live-online planning guide linked above.

## Tools that matter (and tools that can wait)

**Prioritize:**

- A reliable live channel (video or classroom display workflow)
- Interactive activities tied to objectives
- A simple assignment + completion path
- A way to share practice links with families

**Add later:**

- Extra AI generators (after you can edit outputs quickly) — see UNESCO’s [Guidance for generative AI in education and research](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research)
- Complex LMS features you will not use weekly
- Novelty games with no language target

## Classroom management online (kids-specific)

- Teach the “mute / unmute / hand raise / join code” routine explicitly in lesson one  
- Use call-and-response and visual timers  
- Prefer show-me tasks (choose, match, tap) interleaved with speaking  
- Name the language goal in child-friendly words (“Today we ask about hobbies”)  
- Have a backup offline micro-task if someone’s connection drops  

For low-connectivity contexts, TeachingEnglish’s [remote teaching tips](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/publications/resource-books/remote-teaching-tips) cover SMS, radio, phone, and limited-tech options — still relevant for blended programs.

Also useful: [maintaining student motivation while teaching remotely](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/maintaining) and [inclusion in remote teaching contexts](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/inclusion).

## Assessment without killing the energy

Online does not mean only auto-marked quizzes.

Mix:

- Observational notes during live speaking  
- Short interactive checks  
- One recorded or written sample every few lessons  
- Homework completion as *practice evidence*, not the whole grade story  

Cambridge’s online teaching materials emphasize feedback and routines as much as platform buttons — start from their [Teaching English online](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/teaching-online/) page and the [quick-start PDF](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/schools/quick-start-guide-to-teaching-english-online.pdf).

## Parent communication that prevents support tickets

Send families:

- The learning goal in one sentence  
- How long practice should take  
- The exact link or class page to use  
- What “finished” looks like  

Avoid jargon. Many parents are supporting in a second language themselves. Point them to [LearnEnglish Kids](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/) for extra practice that does not depend on understanding your full lesson plan.

## Common failure patterns (and fixes)

| Failure | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Long teacher talk, quiet students | Cap present phase; push interaction earlier ([speaking opportunities guide](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/maximising)) |
| New tool every week | Standardize one activity format for a month |
| Homework unrelated to class | Assign the same target with light variation |
| No review of results | Block 10 minutes pre-class to scan completion/errors |
| Account chaos for kids | Prefer join codes and teacher-managed entry |
| Assuming every home has strong wifi | Plan a low-tech fallback ([remote teaching tips](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/publications/resource-books/remote-teaching-tips)) |

## FAQ

### How long should an online English class be for kids?

Often 30–60 minutes depending on age. Younger learners usually need shorter sessions or more mid-lesson movement between activity types.

### Can online teaching work for absolute beginners?

Yes — with heavy visual support, repetition, and carefully limited language. Avoid text-heavy instructions as the main input. Cambridge young-learner online guidance on their [Teaching English online](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/teaching-online/) page is a good next read.

### Do I need a full LMS on day one?

No. You need a live channel, a practice activity, and a clear assignment path. Grow systems after the weekly routine is stable.

### How is this different from “putting worksheets on Zoom”?

Interactive practice, reusable activities across live and homework, and a review step turn online time into a teaching system — not a webcam delivery of paper tasks. That is also the spirit of UNESCO’s [technology-on-education’s-terms](https://gem-report-2023.unesco.org/technology-in-education/) framing: tools serve pedagogy, not the other way around.

### Should I use AI to write my lessons?

Use it to draft examples or extra items, then edit. Follow [UNESCO GenAI guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research) on human oversight, age-appropriate use, and privacy — especially with children’s data.
