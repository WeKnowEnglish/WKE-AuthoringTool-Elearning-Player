# What is EdTech?

**EdTech** (education technology) is any digital tool, platform, or system used to teach, learn, assess, or run a school or classroom. For English teachers, that can mean a shared slide deck — or a full loop from lesson creation to live teaching, homework, independent practice, and progress review.

This guide keeps the definition practical. You will leave with a clear map of EdTech categories, what matters specifically for ESL/EFL, and a short checklist so “new tool” does not become “new chaos.” For the wider story of how schools are adapting, see [How is technology changing education?](/resources/how-is-technology-changing-education).

## A simple definition (without the buzzwords)

If a product helps students **learn**, teachers **teach**, or schools **operate** — and it runs on digital technology — it is EdTech.

That includes:

- Apps students open on a phone or tablet
- Platforms teachers use to plan and deliver lessons
- Learning management systems (LMS) that hold courses and grades
- Assessment and speech tools
- Communication tools for parents and classes
- AI tutors and generators (when used for education)

It does **not** automatically include every trendy gadget. UNESCO’s [2023 Global Education Monitoring Report on technology in education](https://www.unesco.org/gem-report/en/publication/technology) puts the tension clearly: technology can solve real problems, but only when used on *education’s* terms — not the vendor’s demo script. A tool earns its place if it improves clarity, practice, feedback, access, or teacher time.

## Why English teachers should care

Language learning depends on **input, interaction, output, and feedback**. Technology can amplify each of those — or distract from them.

Used well, EdTech can:

- Give every learner more turns to speak, read, and write
- Make practice available after the live lesson ends
- Show teachers which language targets still need work
- Reduce prep time when activities are reusable across live class, homework, and self-study

Used poorly, it becomes a pile of logins, broken audio, and activities that look modern but do not teach. Cambridge English’s [quick-start guide to teaching English online](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/schools/quick-start-guide-to-teaching-english-online.pdf) (PDF) is blunt about the same point: online teaching works when you plan interaction, routines, and transitions — not when you only move a lecture onto a webcam.

For a workflow-focused view of online English teaching (create → teach → assign → practise → review), see [How to teach English online](/teach-english-online).

## The main categories of EdTech

Teachers do not need to master every category. Knowing the map helps you ask better buying and adoption questions. Industry and research coverage of the sector (for example [EdSurge](https://www.edsurge.com/) and the OECD’s recurring [Digital Education Outlook](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html)) keeps expanding these buckets as AI, assessment, and school operations tools evolve — the labels below are stable enough for classroom decisions.

### 1. Content and activity tools

Interactive lessons, flashcards, quizzes, games, story players, grammar posters, and media libraries. These are often where language teachers live day to day.

**Ask:** Can I author once and reuse the same activity live, for homework, and for independent practice?

**Worth exploring:** free learner practice libraries such as [British Council LearnEnglish Kids](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/) and Cambridge’s [resources for English teachers](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/) — useful both as student practice and as models of clear task design.

### 2. Live teaching and classroom tools

Video classrooms, digital whiteboards, breakout rooms, polls, and join-code session tools.

**Ask:** Does it reduce friction for joining and participating — especially for kids — or add setup stress every lesson?

**Worth exploring:** British Council [remote teaching guidance](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance) (including [lesson planning for live online teaching](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/lesson) and [Zoom tips for English teaching](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/zoom-top-tips)).

### 3. Learning management and assignment systems

Course shells, homework assignment, due dates, progress dashboards, and parent/teacher visibility.

**Ask:** Can I see who completed practice and what they struggled with, without exporting five spreadsheets?

### 4. Assessment and feedback

Quizzes, auto-scoring, rubrics, speech recognition, writing feedback, and mastery tracking.

**Ask:** Does feedback help the learner improve the *language target*, or only produce a score?

### 5. Communication and community

Messaging, announcements, class pages, and shareable practice links for parents.

**Ask:** Is communication clear for families who may not speak English fluently?

British Council TeachingEnglish materials on [helping parents support remote learning](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/publications/resource-books/remote-teaching-tips) are a practical companion here.

### 6. AI-assisted teaching and learning

Lesson drafting, differentiation suggestions, tutoring chatbots, item generation, and translation aids.

**Ask:** Who is accountable for accuracy and age-appropriateness — the teacher, or the model?

UNESCO’s [Guidance for generative AI in education and research](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research) argues for a human-centred approach: protect data privacy, set age-appropriate limits, and keep teachers able to validate tools pedagogically. The OECD’s [Digital Education Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html) adds a classroom-relevant warning — generative AI can raise *task completion* without guaranteeing *learning* unless pedagogy is designed on purpose. Treat AI as a drafting assistant and practice partner, not as the syllabus.

## EdTech that tends to help ESL / EFL classrooms

Language teachers usually get the most value from tools that support:

| Need | What good EdTech looks like |
|------|-----------------------------|
| Listening & speaking | Clear audio, repeatable prompts, low-friction turn-taking |
| Vocabulary & grammar | Short, focused practice tied to a lesson objective |
| Reading & writing | Scaffolded tasks, models, and feedback loops |
| Mixed levels | Easy differentiation without rebuilding the whole lesson |
| Young learners | Simple join paths, visual cues, short activity cycles |
| Teacher time | Author once; reuse across modes |

Browse interactive examples aimed at younger learners on [ESL activities for kids](/esl-activities-for-kids) when that hub is live, or try a free classroom activity from the We Know English homepage. For independent practice families can trust, [LearnEnglish Kids](https://learnenglishkids.britishcouncil.org/) remains one of the strongest free public libraries.

## What EdTech is *not* (common misunderstandings)

- **Not automatically better teaching.** A worksheet on a screen is still a worksheet. The GEM Report’s question — *[a tool on whose terms?](https://gem-report-2023.unesco.org/technology-in-education/)* — is the right filter.
- **Not only AI.** Most effective classroom tech is still structured content, clear routines, and good feedback.
- **Not only for remote school.** Blended and in-person classes use EdTech constantly.
- **Not a replacement for curriculum.** Tools deliver practice; teachers still choose goals, sequence, and standards.
- **Not one platform for every problem.** Schools often need a small stack — but “small” should be intentional.

## How to evaluate a tool in under 15 minutes

Use this checklist before you adopt anything new:

1. **Learning objective** — What language can students do better after using it?
2. **Learner fit** — Age, CEFR/level, device reality (phones? shared tablets? weak wifi?).
3. **Teacher workflow** — Can you find, assign, and review work quickly?
4. **Reuse** — Does one activity travel across live teaching and homework?
5. **Feedback** — Do students get useful correction, not only points?
6. **Privacy & safety** — Especially for children: accounts, data, chat, recordings. (The GEM Report notes how weakly many systems protect learner data — treat privacy as a purchase criterion, not an afterthought.)
7. **Evidence honesty** — Can the vendor point to independent evaluation, or only marketing case studies? GEM 2023 highlights how thin impartial evidence often is.
8. **Exit cost** — If the tool disappears next year, can you export or rebuild essential content?

If a product fails on objective, fit, or privacy, skip it — even if the demo looks impressive. For school-level digital practice frameworks, [ISTE standards and resources](https://www.iste.org/) are a useful shared language with administrators.

## Practical starting stack for many English teachers

You do not need twenty apps. A lean stack often looks like:

1. **One live channel** — video classroom or in-person projector workflow
2. **One activity/practice platform** — reusable interactive lessons
3. **One place for assignments & visibility** — even a simple class page or LMS
4. **One communication path** for families

Add AI or specialized speech tools only after the core loop is stable. Cambridge’s [Teaching English online hub](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/educators-organisations/resources-for-teachers/teaching-online/) is a good place to deepen platform skills without jumping to the next shiny product.

## FAQ

### Is Google Classroom EdTech?

Yes. It is primarily an LMS/assignment and classroom-organization layer. Many teachers pair it with separate activity tools.

### Is YouTube EdTech?

It can be. Video is powerful input — but without tasks, checks for understanding, and follow-up practice, it is just media. See TeachingEnglish guidance on [teaching via TV or YouTube](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/using-digital-technologies/remote-teaching-guidance/teaching-3) for structured approaches.

### Do I need AI to be “modern”?

No. Reliable routines, comprehensible input, and frequent meaningful practice matter more than any single model. UNESCO and OECD guidance both put pedagogy and human oversight first.

### What should I learn first if I feel behind?

Start with the tool you already use for live lessons, then add **one** practice/assignment layer you can reuse weekly. Depth beats novelty. The British Council’s free [Teaching Young Learners Online](https://www.futurelearn.com/courses/teaching-young-learners-online) course (FutureLearn) is a solid structured on-ramp.
