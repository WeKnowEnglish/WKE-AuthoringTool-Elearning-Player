# WeKnow English unit-builder guide

Version 1.0 · 10 October 2026 · Primary users: curriculum authors and teachers

**Keep the learning commitments consistent; adapt the route, time and support.** A unit is a connected learning journey in which students understand and use language for a purpose that matters to them. Its length follows the learners and the task, rather than a fixed lesson count.

Use the [planning template](./unit-planning-template.md) when creating or revising a unit. The requirements below are authoring guidelines and review checks; the website does not yet enforce them automatically.

## 1. The commitments every unit makes

Every unit must include:

1. A meaningful situation, an understandable purpose and an age-appropriate audience.
2. A learner starting point: relevant experience, language prerequisites and a brief entry check.
3. Observable objectives and success criteria linked to course outcomes and appropriate CEFR descriptors.
4. **Six distinct dedicated lessons:** reading, writing, listening, speaking, vocabulary and grammar. Each has one primary focus, a taught strategy or language purpose, meaningful practice and its own learning check.
5. An introduction that activates prior learning and previews the unit purpose and final task.
6. A planned movement from modelling and guided practice toward active understanding and independent communication.
7. Cumulative review, feedback, a chance to improve and an individual contribution to a transfer checkpoint.
8. Spaced retrieval within the unit and in named later units, reusing language in broader contexts.
9. A bridge to the next unit's prerequisites, with a follow-up plan for gaps.
10. Adaptation choices that preserve the common learning purpose while adjusting access, support and challenge.

Dedicated lessons can integrate other skills: a reading lesson may include partner talk; a grammar lesson must use meaning and communication. It counts toward **one** of the six primary focuses. Six skill tags on a mixed lesson do not replace six lessons. Vocabulary and grammar are dedicated language-system lessons alongside the four communicative skills. Pronunciation, phonics, interaction and mediation are taught where needed throughout the unit, with additional support lessons when useful.

The introduction, review and transfer checkpoint normally deserve their own lessons. They may be substantial, explicitly timed parts of dedicated lessons when the class is ready and those lessons retain their main learning purpose. They must remain identifiable in the plan. There is no universal nine-lesson rule, fixed sequence or fixed duration; six distinct focus lessons are the lower bound, not a recommended target to compress toward.

## 2. Begin with students' lives and a purposeful outcome

Write a short learner profile before choosing activities: age, interests, home languages, current performance by skill, relevant experiences, literacy, confidence, access needs and available class time. Age and language level are separate. A learner can understand an age-appropriate idea while needing simpler English to express it.

Choose a context students can recognise, influence or imagine: making a class guide, helping a visitor, choosing a game, organising a shared space, explaining a discovery or solving a fictional mystery. Offer personal, local and imaginary versions. A home unit can use an invented floor plan; a shopping unit can use pretend money. Participation should not depend on family spending, travel experience or personal disclosure.

Give students meaningful bounded choices: topic, example, character, partner question, design or audience. Retain a shared objective. Ask, **“Who needs this message, and what will they do with it?”** If the answer is only “the teacher will mark it,” strengthen the purpose.

## 3. Plan backward from evidence

Define the final performance first, then the knowledge, language and strategies that make it possible. Select a manageable set of unit objectives; three to five is a useful starting point, not a quota. Add lesson-level objectives for the six focuses, linked to those unit goals, rather than inflating the unit list to match every activity.

For each objective record:

- A communicative can-do, a learner-friendly version and the relevant course objective.
- CEFR level, descriptor scale and source reference where appropriate. Adapt descriptors to the age and task; grammar topics alone do not determine a CEFR level.
- The evidence: what a learner will interpret, choose, explain, ask, write or do.
- Success criteria, permitted support and what the teacher will observe individually.
- The next action after partial success: feedback, focused practice and a different recheck.

Example: **“Give directions that help a partner reach a service on a simple map.”** Check the starting point, ordered route, useful landmarks and whether the listener reaches the destination. Record repetitions or map cues. Fluent recitation of a memorised route is different evidence from responding to a new destination.

Use both a reception task and a production/interaction task when the unit goals require both. One project cannot automatically demonstrate every skill. A unit's results describe demonstrated objectives and support used; they do not certify an overall CEFR level.

## 4. Give each dedicated lesson a clear job

| Primary focus | What must be taught and practised | Suitable evidence | Adaptable support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reading | Read a connected text for a purpose; model a strategy such as predicting, finding a detail or checking a reference. | Use information from a fresh accessible text to match, order, decide or answer. | Chunking, picture glossary, readable layout, rereading; phonics support when needed. |
| Listening | Listen to a meaningful message; teach listening for gist, a relevant detail, sequence or clarification. | Act on, sequence or select information from fresh audio or live speech. | Preview the situation, replay, slower clear delivery, visuals; record support. |
| Speaking | Exchange or present meaning to a listener; teach turn-taking, relevant questions, repair and intelligibility. | An individual contribution in a short exchange or talk with a relevant follow-up. | Rehearsal, role cards, keyword prompts, supportive partners and wait time. |
| Writing | Plan, compose, check and improve a message for a reader. | An individual text plus a useful revision; copying a model alone is insufficient. | Shared model, planning grid, word bank, oral rehearsal and a focused checklist. |
| Vocabulary | Connect meaning, spoken/written form and useful chunks; retrieve and use a selected core set in context. | Retrieve meaning or form, then select/use words appropriately in a meaningful task. | Images, objects, examples, sound–spelling work and contrast with familiar words. |
| Grammar | Connect form, meaning and communicative function; notice a pattern, practise it and choose it for a purpose. | Interpret a meaningful contrast and use the pattern to communicate a relevant message. | Concrete examples, visual timelines, substitution practice, explanation suited to age. |

Select vocabulary and grammar because the unit task needs them. Separate a small productive core from words learners only need to recognise and optional enrichment. The appropriate amount depends on familiarity, word complexity and reuse opportunities; do not impose the same word count on every unit.

Listening is not just reading a transcript, speaking is not only repeating, and grammar is not only filling gaps. During supported learning, transcripts and models can help. For a listening check, use audio first and record whether a transcript was provided. A reading or listening check may use pointing, ordering or acting so a writing difficulty does not hide understanding.

## 5. Build flexible paths through the unit

Design the path around dependencies. Vocabulary may precede reading, or an accessible story may introduce a vocabulary need. Listening may provide a model before speaking; oral rehearsal may help writing. Revisit these processes rather than assuming each appears only once.

| Lesson path | Purpose | When to use it | What must be explicit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Teach the shared objectives and all six dedicated focuses. | Every learner receives this learning entitlement. | Objective, task, evidence and sequence dependency. |
| Support | Remove a barrier or reteach a diagnosed gap. | Evidence shows a learner/group needs it. | Entry trigger, targeted help and a recheck/return point. |
| Optional extension | Deepen an already accessible core outcome. | Readiness and interest justify it. | Added challenge and an equivalent route through the core. |

A support lesson is conditional, but it becomes necessary for learners who need that support. It may be a whole-class addition or a small-group workshop. An optional extension must not introduce a prerequisite that the next core unit silently assumes everyone learned.

Teachers may change order, examples, timing, grouping, text length and scaffolds; add more grammar, vocabulary, literacy or strategy lessons; or remove unnecessary additional practice. They retain the six dedicated focuses, shared outcomes, required milestones and valid evidence. A short entry check can shorten practice for ready learners, but it does not replace a dedicated lesson's strategy, meaningful task and check.

### Example A: identity unit — nine core lessons

| Lesson | Primary purpose | Progression and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Introduction and entry check | Use the existing welcome lesson; activate greetings and personal information and preview a learner profile. |
| 2 | Vocabulary | Learn/retrieve core interests, strengths and routine chunks through pictures and personally meaningful choices. |
| 3 | Listening | Interpret short learner profiles for useful details; move from teacher modelling to a fresh listening check. |
| 4 | Grammar | Connect present simple and frequency expressions to routine meaning; interpret and then form relevant statements/questions. |
| 5 | Reading | Find and compare routine details in short profiles, using a taught locating/checking strategy. |
| 6 | Speaking | Interview a partner, clarify one detail and respond from keywords rather than a complete script. |
| 7 | Writing | Plan, compose and revise a learner profile for a new classmate; collect individual writing evidence. |
| 8 | Cumulative review and improvement | Retrieve earlier greetings and current language, apply them to different profiles and respond to feedback. |
| 9 | Transfer checkpoint and next-unit bridge | Interpret a fresh profile and share a revised personal/fictional profile with follow-up questions; prepare school routines/time language for Unit 2. |

Add a conditional vocabulary/phonics workshop before lesson 3 if the entry evidence warrants it. Offer an optional class-profile gallery with further questions after the core. This is one possible path, not the standard order for every unit.

### Example B: past mystery unit — eleven core lessons plus optional support

| Lesson | Primary purpose | Why this path differs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Introduction and retrieval | Establish a familiar classroom mystery; retrieve known people, places and present-time routines. |
| 2 | Vocabulary | Build a small core of clue, action and time chunks needed to follow the mystery. |
| 3 | Reading | Model using who/where/when and a timeline to interpret a short clue account. |
| 4 | Grammar | Contrast present and past meaning; work with was/were and a small useful verb set. |
| 5 | Listening | Follow a fresh witness account and place events; check understanding without demanding an extended written answer. |
| 6 | Additional grammar | Teach did questions and useful regular/irregular verbs through witness information gaps. |
| 7 | Speaking | Exchange witness information, clarify and distinguish a clue from a guess. |
| 8 | Writing | Plan and revise an individual ordered recount, drawing on earlier location language. |
| 9 | Additional vocabulary and pronunciation | Retrieve action chunks after a delay and practise intelligible past endings in meaningful short accounts. |
| 10 | Cumulative review and improvement | Solve a different short case using earlier and current language; target misconceptions. |
| 11 | Transfer checkpoint and next-unit bridge | Interpret fresh evidence and give an individual recount/exchange; retrieve familiar numbers and polite requests before the shopping unit. |

Insert a conditional timeline/reading workshop after lesson 3 for learners who need it. Offer an optional alternative-ending challenge using familiar language. The greater grammar load justifies extra lessons without demanding a higher CEFR level.

## 6. Move from guided to independent learning

Plan at both unit and lesson level:

| Phase | Teacher contribution | Learner contribution | Check before reducing support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Model and make meaning clear | Demonstrate a useful response and think aloud about the strategy. | Notice, interpret and connect it to an experience or example. | Can the learner show what it means? |
| Guided practice | Give frames, prompts, feedback and manageable choices. | Try the strategy or pattern with help. | Is success more than copying the visible answer? |
| Supported application | Provide partial cues and a new information gap or familiar variation. | Select information, make choices and repair misunderstandings. | Can the learner use the learning when an example changes? |
| Independent application | Give the purpose, conditions and agreed access tools. | Interpret and produce a relevant response, making meaningful choices. | What did the individual understand or communicate, with what support? |
| Feedback and transfer | Give focused feedback and a different later opportunity. | Improve, explain a choice and apply learning in a broader context. | Does the learning survive a delay and variation? |

Independence means the learner makes the central meaning and language decisions. It does not require removing a necessary access aid or forbidding the visuals, repetitions and preparation appropriate to the descriptor. Reduce answer-giving scaffolds gradually; record the remaining support. Move back to a targeted model when needed, then return to application.

## 7. Balance difficulty on separate dimensions

Active understanding means using meaning to make a decision, connect details, follow an instruction, detect a mismatch or justify a simple choice. It can be cognitively demanding even with familiar words. Independent production adds selection, organisation and response demands even when the grammar stays the same.

Plan these dimensions separately. Do not raise all of them at once.

| Dimension | Ways difficulty can increase | Ways to hold it manageable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language | Less familiar words, more complex clauses or a new function. | Reuse known chunks; introduce only language the task needs. |
| Meaning and thinking | More details to connect, less obvious information, explanation or competing choices. | Familiar context, visible facts and a clear question. |
| Independence | Fewer frames, less rehearsal, more learner decisions. | Keep the language and context familiar while fading prompts. |
| Text and processing | Longer text/audio, denser information, faster delivery or less preparation time. | Chunk information, allow suitable replay/rereading and time. |
| Interaction and context | New partner, unfamiliar variation or an unexpected follow-up. | Introduce one variation after success in a familiar setting. |

Example using the same A2-oriented comparison language: first choose which timetable starts earlier; next find and explain the difference from two timetables; later explain it to a partner without sentence frames. If independence increases, keep vocabulary, text length and context familiar. Optional stretch can add a reason or unexpected question without automatically adding a new tense or CEFR level.

A task can allow an easier entry and richer response: everyone gives a useful route; some add a landmark, compare alternatives or answer a follow-up. Record the particular achievement rather than assigning a fixed ability label.

## 8. Make review cumulative and repetition spaced

Review is a planned opportunity to retrieve, diagnose and apply. Spaced repetition means returning after a delay; repeatedly displaying the same answer in one lesson is not a spacing plan. Use the following as a local scheduling starting point, then adjust to the timetable and evidence:

| Return | What to plan | What to record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction day | Meaningful input, guided use and an initial check. | Target and first teaching point. |
| Next lesson | A brief retrieval attempt before showing the model, followed by feedback. | Response and help needed. |
| Two or three lessons later | Retrieve the target in a different task within the unit. | Whether meaning/form remain available. |
| Unit review/checkpoint | Apply a selection of old and new targets together. | Gaps requiring focused practice. |
| Following unit and roughly one or two weeks later | Use the target in a named broader context; align dates to actual teaching. | Planned lesson/date, actual return and response. |
| A later cumulative unit, roughly three to six weeks later | Recheck key targets with a fresh familiar task. | Retention and next review action. |

These intervals and brief retrieval starters of about three to five minutes are adjustable WKE planning defaults, not research-prescribed optimums or a requirement to test every target in every lesson. Include at least one delayed within-unit return and one named later-unit return for each priority target. Continue important targets beyond the next unit.

Let learners attempt retrieval, give timely feedback, then try again. Mix current and selected earlier learning without overloading the task. Some returns should require meaningful spoken/written use or active comprehension rather than recognition alone. Homework can supplement this plan; provide in-class retrieval for learners whose home access is limited.

Maintain a small target ledger: **target ID → introduce → guided practice → delayed retrieval → assessment → later return**. Examples across Upper Primary 1: routines in school schedules and wellbeing logs; home prepositions in mystery clues and city maps; comparisons in shopping choices and inventions; advice and responsibilities in environmental action. Later units must explicitly use the recycled language, rather than only naming it in a review list.

## 9. Adapt from evidence and prepare the next unit

Use entry checks, focus-lesson checks and exit evidence to decide:

| Evidence | Teacher response |
| --- | --- |
| Meaning is unclear across much of the class. | Reteach with concrete examples, reduce new language and insert a core/support lesson. |
| A small group needs a specific vocabulary, grammar, decoding or listening strategy. | Give a targeted workshop while others apply the same core outcome; recheck the targeted skill. |
| Learners succeed only while reading a full model. | Retain familiar language, reduce to partial cues and check a new example. |
| Reception succeeds but speaking/writing does not. | Provide rehearsal, formulation and production practice; retain the reception evidence separately. |
| Learners communicate clearly after a delay with agreed support. | Reduce redundant extra practice, broaden context or offer optional extension. |

Use a simple evidence description: **not yet demonstrated; achieved with substantial modelling; achieved with agreed support; achieved with greater independence in a new familiar task**. Keep the task, date and support alongside the judgement. Confidence, speed, participation and a single quiz score are insufficient evidence on their own. Set task-specific success criteria in advance; there is no universal percentage cut-score for moving on.

At the unit exit, identify the next unit's actual entry needs. Briefly retrieve or introduce the relevant everyday context; do not front-load its entire new grammar syllabus. Record which prerequisites are secure and which need a catch-up route. Move the class forward with targeted follow-up where possible, rather than keeping every learner in repeated whole-unit reteaching. Offer a delayed recheck after feedback.

Keep personal adjustments in the class plan. A parent update can explain one demonstrated can-do, the support used and one useful next step in plain language. Curriculum leaders use recurring gaps to revise the shared unit, rather than treating one class's pace as a permanent rule.

## 10. Connect the guide to the website

| Planning decision | Existing place to record/use it |
| --- | --- |
| Learners, course outcomes and entry expectations | Course details in Libraries → Course map. |
| Unit purpose, final task, review and next-unit bridge | Unit outcome; keep the fuller design in the planning template. |
| Six dedicated focuses | One distinct planned lesson for each; identify its primary focus in its title/task and use skill tags for its actual activities. Multiple tags do not identify a single primary focus automatically. |
| Can-dos, criteria and evidence | Reusable objectives and each lesson's objective links. |
| Introduction, support/optional status, release conditions and progression | Lesson title and teacher-led task; these statuses are currently manual planning conventions. |
| Language, dependencies and adaptations | Target language, prerequisite lesson links, support and extension fields. Prerequisites are planning references, not access locks. |
| Spaced returns | Stable targets with introduce/practise/assess/revisit roles on the lessons that actually do that work; dates and decisions in the class plan/ledger. |
| Source texts, audio, vocabulary and activities | Existing Libraries resources, linked to the planned lesson where supported; prepare teacher-led materials where needed. |
| Teaching a specific class | Create a class lesson draft from the saved map, then adapt steps, time, materials and checks in the planner. |

The existing import carries objectives, checks, target language, minutes, a teacher-led task and support/extension notes, and records the source revision. Selected vocabulary can be copied; other resource links remain planning references according to the current pilot. Review and prepare materials before releasing/assigning. Later map changes do not silently rewrite an imported class plan.

The current map does **not** automatically enforce the six-lesson rule, choose support paths, schedule spaced review, score every objective, update parent reports or personalise mascot practice. Use the template and teacher review now; future tooling should support these decisions using actual learner evidence.

## 11. Review a unit before calling it ready to teach

- [ ] The context, purpose and choices suit students' lives, ages, abilities and access needs.
- [ ] Every objective has observable evidence, success criteria and recorded support conditions.
- [ ] Six distinct reading, writing, listening, speaking, vocabulary and grammar lessons have a clear primary purpose and individual check.
- [ ] Introduction, cumulative review, improvement and an individual transfer checkpoint have identifiable time and tasks.
- [ ] Guidance decreases toward active understanding and independent communication; difficulty does not rise on every dimension at once.
- [ ] Core language is usable across tasks; optional extension introduces no hidden next-unit prerequisite.
- [ ] Conditional support has a trigger, targeted task and return/recheck point.
- [ ] Priority targets have delayed within-unit and named later-unit returns in a ledger.
- [ ] Earlier course language is actually used in broader contexts, and the next unit's entry needs are checked.
- [ ] Sources, audio, questions/answers, examples and materials are prepared and checked; manual classroom tasks have clear instructions.
- [ ] Class-specific adjustments, access tools, expected timings and next actions are recorded.

A unit may be a useful **draft** while some boxes remain open. The checklist is an authoring review gate, not a claim about student mastery or an automatic software restriction.

## Sources and the limits of the guidance

The [Council of Europe action-oriented approach](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/the-action-oriented-approach) supports purposeful scenarios that connect reception, production, interaction and mediation, with learner choice and teacher support. Use the [CEFR Companion Volume](https://rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4) for descriptor alignment, and the young-learner collections for [ages 7–10](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1688) and [11–15](https://rm.coe.int/16808b1689) for age-relevant adaptation.

The IES practice guide [Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1) supports distributed review and active retrieval with feedback. Its evidence spans content-learning contexts; the language-unit schedules here are local applications to be adjusted after classroom use.

The six distinct focus lessons, flexible milestone placement, example sequences, timing suggestions and readiness checklist are **WKE curriculum decisions** based on this project's teaching goals. They are not prescribed CEFR unit structures or claims that one sequence suits every learner.
